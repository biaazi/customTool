Planet one · The lantern

Headspace privacy redesign

For people trusting an app with their inner weather.

Role
UX design
Year
2024
Tools
Figma

Who lives here

People who open a meditation app at 11pm, tired, wanting quiet. Before they have taken a single breath they are asked to hand over data, and the ask arrives in the language of a contract.

What they needed

To understand what they are agreeing to, in plain words, at the moment it matters. A privacy flow that respects the same autonomy the product promises everywhere else.

What I made

A rebuilt onboarding where permissions arrive one at a time, each with a reason, a real choice, and nothing hidden behind a scroll. The tone matches the app: calm, unhurried, honest. Every permission can be declined and the first session still opens.

What changed

Consent became part of the experience instead of a gate in front of it. Privacy stopped feeling like a mood killer and started feeling like the app keeping its word.

Planet two · The compass

Roam & Rest travel app

For travellers deciding where to sleep tonight, from a phone, in transit.

Role
UX and UI design
Year
2025
Tools
Figma

Who lives here

Travellers comparing dozens of listings on a small screen, often half distracted, often on a deadline set by a train timetable.

What they needed

Filters that actually narrow things down, and listing pages that answer the real questions first: where is it, how much is it, and what is it like to be there.

What I made

A search flow built around a customizable filter modal, a results page that stays scannable as it fills up, and a property detail screen that puts decision-making information above the fold.

What changed

Fewer taps between "I need a room" and "this is the one." The experience became a guide rather than a catalogue.

Planet three · The veil

Migraine Buddy accessibility redesign

For people who need an app they can use with their eyes half closed.

Role
UX research and design
Year
2025
Tools
Figma

Who lives here

People living with migraine. Light hurts, contrast can hurt, and logging an attack usually happens in the middle of one.

What they needed

Clear navigation, visual settings they can adjust themselves, and a recording flow that asks for as little as possible when they have the least to give.

What I made

A redesign led by accessibility research: simplified navigation, customizable contrast and brightness, and a cohesive visual identity that stays gentle across the home, discover and tracking screens.

What changed

Tracking became something you can do during an attack rather than after it. The interface adapts to the person, not the other way around.
